# Learning-Product Prior Art — Web Research Report

All claims cite URLs. Organized by the six questions, ending with consolidated steal/refuse lists.

## 1. Language-from-content tools (LingQ, Readlang, Language Reactor, Migaku)

**Core shared mechanic — the known-word ledger.** LingQ tags every token in a lesson by familiarity: blue = never seen, yellow = "LingQed" (learning), white = known; a known word can be demoted back to learning with one click (https://www.lingq.com/blog/lingq-review/, https://www.lingq.com/blog/reviewing-vocabulary/). This per-word state, persisted across all content, is what makes "read anything and the app knows what's new *to you*" work. LingQ reviews words via SRS with five activities (flashcards, reverse, cloze, dictation, multiple choice) (https://www.lingq.com/blog/reviewing-vocabulary/). Every lesson pairs text with native-speaker audio — read, listen, or both — which is the closest existing thing to the "stop in one format, resume in another" idea (https://www.lingq.com/blog/lingq-review/).

**Readlang** is the minimal version: an eReader where clicking a word (or click-dragging a phrase) translates it in place, and every translation automatically becomes a flashcard carrying original context, translation, and source; its SRS uniquely uses corpus word-frequency as a scheduling input, so common words surface sooner (https://readlang.com/features, https://www.mezzoguild.com/readlang-review/, https://www.lingualift.com/blog/read-more-languages-with-readlang-review/).

**Language Reactor** overlays Netflix/YouTube with dual subtitles, click-to-define, auto-pause after each subtitle line, and slowdown; export and deeper dictionaries are behind a paid Pro tier (https://www.classcentral.com/report/review-language-reactor/, https://funfluen.com/learn/compare/language-reactor-vs-migaku/). **Migaku**'s one-click card capture is the gold-standard mining UX: one click saves sentence + audio + screenshot + translation into a flashcard deck, exportable to Anki (https://funfluen.com/learn/compare/language-reactor-vs-migaku/).

**What requires a server:** LingQ is server-bound — users report that losing WiFi means no lessons at all, a long-standing complaint (https://forum.lingq.com/t/offline-mode-for-android/27278). Migaku requires an account and subscription; its offline story is partial (flashcards sync media for offline study, but the pipeline is account-gated) (https://www.bestaitools.com/tool/migaku/, https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.migaku.android). Nothing in the *mechanics* (per-word state, click-to-define, context capture) inherently needs a server — the server dependence is dictionaries/MT lookups and account sync, both replaceable with on-device dictionaries and local models. Sentence mining itself, as the community defines it, is just "whole sentences from content you consume, kept as flashcards with meaningful context" (https://forum.lingq.com/t/what-is-sentence-mining/35586).

## 2. SuperMemo incremental reading

**Mechanics worth stealing** (from SuperMemo's own docs): (a) read many articles *incrementally* — every article is a queue element, you read a portion and move on (https://help.supermemo.org/wiki/Incremental_reading); (b) **extracts** (Alt+X) split articles into smaller child portions that inherit context and get their own review schedule; **clozes** (Alt+Z) turn a sentence into Q/A via deletion (https://www.super-memory.com/archive/help15/read.htm); (c) a **priority queue** where every element carries a 0–100% priority and review order is priority-sorted with deliberate randomization mixed in (https://help.supermemo.org/wiki/Priority_queue); (d) *incremental* cloze generation — don't make all clozes from an extract at once; make one now, another at next review (https://help.supermemo.org/wiki/Priority_queue).

**Known failure modes, from SuperMemo's own documentation and secondary analysis:** too much randomization unravels prioritization and drops high-priority retention; strict priority sorting lets new material permanently displace old (https://help.supermemo.org/wiki/Priority_queue). The priority scale itself is a usability trap — "nearly everyone asks" why top priority is 0% not 100%, and SuperMemo's docs openly admit the software "makes life of beginners hard because it does not compromise learning efficiency for sleekness" (https://super-memory.com/help/il_full.htm). Independent empirical validation of IR's claimed gains is thin — mostly anecdote and SuperMemo's own user data — and the technique demands heavy up-front processing investment (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incremental_reading, https://grokipedia.com/page/Incremental_reading). Lesson: steal extracts + priorities + interleaving, but hide the machinery behind defaults; never expose a 0-is-highest percent scale.

## 3. Mnemonic medium (Matuschak/Nielsen) + the memory literature

**Quantum Country / Orbit.** The text breaks every few hundred words for embedded review prompts; days later you're re-prompted, on an efficiently expanding schedule (days → weeks → months) (https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Mnemonic_medium, https://quantum.country/). Orbit is the general-purpose platform for writing in this medium (https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z4RTpQqeT7JkxyaV7WwBJ2b). Matuschak's notes stress that the medium "gives structure to normally-atomized prompts" — prompts live inside a narrative, not as orphan cards (https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z8e98xn7t5QNHr5cf8oqjbJ) — and warns explicitly against "orphan prompts" disconnected from context (https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zKPv6qkSErdRGqyryvgS2wS?stackedNotes=z8QtbrR4cxDshTYBq3RCfwUVkXS8mSjRmAnqk).

**Prompt-writing principles** from "How to write good prompts" (https://andymatuschak.org/prompts/): good prompts are **focused** (one detail), **precise** ("vague questions elicit vague answers"), **consistent** (same answer every time, else retrieval-induced forgetting inhibits neighbors), **tractable** (~90% accuracy), and **effortful** (must actually retrieve from memory — recognition isn't retrieval). Anti-patterns named there: long cloze passages that train pattern-matching on the question's *shape*; binary yes/no prompts (minimal effort, shallow); orphan facts with multiple defensible answers. It also invokes the **generation effect** — "you remember information better when you generated it yourself" — recommending prompts that ask the reader to *generate* novel examples, and endorses writing your own prompts to build "understandings of your own" beyond the author's (https://andymatuschak.org/prompts/). This is the direct answer to "highlighted flashcards had no construction behind them": the fix is prompts embedded in the discourse, authored with these properties, plus a generation step for the reader.

**Literature anchor:** Dunlosky et al. 2013 (*Psychological Science in the Public Interest*) rates **practice testing (retrieval) and distributed practice as high-utility**, **elaborative interrogation** (generating "why/how" explanations) and interleaving as moderate-utility/promising, and **highlighting and rereading as low-utility** (https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1529100612453266, https://www.aft.org/ae/fall2013/dunlosky). Retrieval's long-term-retention benefit is among the best-replicated effects in the field (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959475217301810). So: a study feature built on highlight-then-flip-cards sits on the two *worst*-rated techniques; one built on retrieval + spacing + "why does this follow?" prompts sits on the best.

## 4. FSRS / Anki ecosystem norms

FSRS is now native in Anki (23.10+), replacing SM-2, achieving equal retention with ~15–20% fewer reviews (https://studycardsai.com/blog/anki-fsrs-algorithm, https://faqs.ankiweb.net/frequently-asked-questions-about-fsrs.html). The single user-facing knob serious users expect is **desired retention** (allowed 0.70–0.99; 0.90 recommended for most) (https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/blob/main/docs/tutorial.md, https://faqs.ankiweb.net/frequently-asked-questions-about-fsrs.html). FSRS models each card as (difficulty, stability, retrievability), and parameters are **optimized per-user from their own review log** (https://expertium.github.io/Algorithm.html). Ecosystem norms: `.apkg` deck export/import and `.colpkg` collection export are the lingua franca; review history must survive export because the optimizer feeds on it (https://faqs.ankiweb.net/frequently-asked-questions-about-fsrs.html). Practical implication: ship FSRS (it's MIT-licensed, `open-spaced-repetition`), expose desired-retention as the one setting, keep the full review log locally, and support `.apkg` both ways — serious SRS users treat lock-in of review history as disqualifying.

## 5. Attention sovereignty

**Zvi's "Out to Get You"** (original at https://thezvi.wordpress.com/2017/09/23/out-to-get-you/): a product is Out to Get You when it seeks your resources — time, attention, money — at your expense, and the key variable is whether its demand is **bounded**. Facebook and free-to-play games are the canonical *unbounded* threats ("wants your entire life"; "can charge tens of thousands"). The four responses: **Get Gone**, **Get Got** (accept, *on purpose*, knowing your maximum loss), **Get Compact** (find a bounded rule that caps exposure, then relax), **Get Ready** (permanent battle-mode vigilance — itself costly: "your brain cannot relax"). Design translation: a trustworthy learning app must present a **bounded, legible ask** — a session has a natural end, the app never escalates its demand — so users can Get Compact instead of Get Ready. The companion essay "Slack" makes the complementary point that systems consuming all slack are corrosive (https://thezvi.substack.com/p/slack).

**Cozy web / digital gardens / RSS:** Venkatesh Rao's "cozy web" names the retreat into non-performative, non-algorithmic spaces (https://playeroneuniverse.medium.com/introducing-the-cozy-web-a-sanctuary-amidst-the-dark-forest-9ce08ce0bec2). Digital gardens are non-linear, evolving, tended-not-fed spaces — "an end to the infinite scroll" (https://www.cccb.org/en/w/articles/digitals-gardens-an-end-to-the-infinite-scroll). The RSS framing is exact: the algorithmic timeline is "a slot machine… optimized for compulsion, not comprehension," while an RSS workflow is "a garden where you choose what to plant, tend it on your schedule, and nothing appears that you didn't select" (https://www.nutshellnewsletter.com/blog/what-is-rss-and-why-it-still-matters); Doctorow's "The web is bearable with RSS" argues the same (https://pluralistic.net/2026/03/07/reader-mode/).

**Duolingo-style dark patterns to refuse:** streaks with paid "repair" (loss aversion as the foundational lever of the whole product) (https://medium.com/@productbrief/duolingos-gamified-growth-how-a-green-owl-turned-language-learning-into-a-14-billion-habit-d47d9fa30a77); guilt-tripping push notifications and mascot shaming (https://opinionsandconditions.substack.com/p/duolingo-owl-dark-patterns-digital-guilt); streak anxiety and compulsive checking displacing the actual learning goal, "Duolingo burnout" (https://dev.to/yaptech/duolingos-shallow-learning-trap-gamified-streaks-harmful-habits-4134); "streak creep" — the streak becomes the point, and metric-keeping crowds out the goal (https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/consumer-insights/streak-creep-the-perils-of-too-much-gamification); competitive leaderboards and appointment mechanics resembling addictive social media (https://dev.to/yaptech/duolingos-shallow-learning-trap-gamified-streaks-harmful-habits-4134). **What legitimately helps:** bite-size sessions and immediately visible progress are consistently credited as the *non-manipulative* part of Duolingo's effectiveness (https://medium.com/@navsrujan-mit/duolingo-gamifying-language-learning-eb8e6a55b521); Readlang's fixed-size review sessions (8/15/25 words) are a bounded-session exemplar (https://www.mezzoguild.com/readlang-review/); FSRS's due-count is itself honest visible progress. The mnemonic medium's expanding schedule is an *appointment the memory math sets*, not one engagement metrics set (https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Mnemonic_medium).

## 6. Podcast-first language learning today

What people actually do: run Whisper (or faster-whisper) locally to generate transcripts/SRT from any audio (https://wellstsai.com/en/post/whisper/, https://localaimaster.com/blog/local-ai-subtitles-whisper); use WhisperX's wav2vec2 forced alignment when timing matters — it tightens word boundaries to roughly ±50 ms, "the difference between subtitles glued to the speech and ones that drift" (https://wellstsai.com/en/post/whisper/); or use packaged players — SubX Player already does fully-offline on-device Whisper/Moonshine subtitle generation with dual subtitles, shadowing, auto-repeat, and sentence mining for local files and podcasts (https://www.subx.app/), and Immersive Translate does it server-side in the browser (https://immersivetranslate.com/en/video/podcast-subtitle-generator/).

**Where it breaks:** (1) **hallucination** — ~1% of Whisper transcriptions contain entirely fabricated phrases, 38% of which carry explicit harms, concentrated in silences and disfluent speech ("Careless Whisper," https://arxiv.org/html/2402.08021v2); mitigation is VAD preprocessing (https://github.com/openai/whisper/discussions/679) or hallucination-suppressed variants (Calm-Whisper, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.12969). (2) **Timestamps** — non-English audio yields wrong/short segment timings and repeated hallucinations (https://github.com/openai/whisper/discussions/1862); verbatim-accurate timing needs specialized models (CrisperWhisper, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.16589). (3) **Low-resource languages** need domain fine-tunes (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.15284). (4) The workflow is **fragmented across tools** — transcribe here, align there, mine into Anki elsewhere — which is exactly the seam a unified local-first app closes.

## Consolidated: patterns to steal
1. Per-word known/learning/new state painted over all content (LingQ) — the engine of "study anything."
2. Click/tap-to-define with automatic context-carrying card creation (Readlang, Migaku's one-click sentence+audio+screenshot capture).
3. Paired text+audio per item with resume-in-either-format (LingQ lessons).
4. Extracts + incremental cloze + priority-with-randomization interleaving (SuperMemo) — behind invisible defaults.
5. Prompts embedded in the reading flow on an expanding schedule; authored to be focused/precise/consistent/tractable/effortful; generation-effect prompts (Matuschak, Quantum Country).
6. Retrieval + spacing + elaborative interrogation as the pedagogical spine; treat highlighting as capture, never as study (Dunlosky 2013).
7. FSRS with a single desired-retention knob, per-user optimization from the local review log, `.apkg` in/out.
8. Bounded-session design (fixed-size reviews, natural stopping points) so users can "Get Compact" (Zvi); tend-not-feed garden framing; user-chosen queue à la RSS.
9. Local Whisper + forced alignment + VAD as the on-device transcript pipeline; on-device subtitle generation is proven shippable (SubX).

## Consolidated: patterns to refuse
1. Streaks as hostage + paid streak repair + loss-aversion scaffolding (Duolingo).
2. Guilt notifications, mascot shaming, appointment mechanics set by engagement rather than by memory math.
3. Leaderboards/competitive social pressure in a study tool.
4. Unbounded asks: infinite feeds, "just one more" queues with no natural end (Zvi's unbounded-demand criterion).
5. Server-gated core function: LingQ's no-WiFi-no-lessons failure; account-gated pipelines (Migaku); export behind a paywall (Language Reactor Pro).
6. SuperMemo's exposed machinery: 0%-is-highest priority scales, pro-optimized UI hostile to beginners, mandatory manual prioritization.
7. Highlight-and-flip flashcards as the study model (low-utility per Dunlosky; orphan prompts per Matuschak).
8. Trusting raw Whisper output blind — silence hallucination and timestamp drift require VAD/alignment before showing a learner a transcript as ground truth.
